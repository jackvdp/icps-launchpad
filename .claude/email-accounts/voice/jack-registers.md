# How Jack writes, by recipient

Derived from a read of the ICPS Exchange **Sent Items** mailbox (24,263 messages) sampled across
September 2021 to September 2026, covering internal colleagues, co-host officials, sponsors,
speakers, partners, suppliers, delegates and applicants.

This file is the **register map**. It sits alongside [`jack.md`](jack.md), which describes his
*warm reactive* voice in detail. That file is accurate for replies to people he knows. It is not
the whole picture, and one of its rules is wrong as a blanket instruction. See "The opener
correction" below.

---

## The one correction to jack.md

[`jack.md`](jack.md) says: *"Never open with 'I hope this email finds you well'."*

That holds for **replies inside a live thread with someone he knows**. It does not hold generally.
In outbound mail, first contact, and any email that opens a new subject, the pleasantry opener is
his single most consistent habit. Verbatim, across all five years:

- "I hope you're well." (most frequent)
- "I hope you're both well."

The real rule is narrower: **he does not open with a pleasantry when he is reacting to something.**
If the other person has just said something, he responds to it first. If he is starting the
conversation, he asks after them first. Both are him.

## Register map

### Tracy Drewett (line manager, peer)
Shortest register he has. No pleasantry, often no more than a clause. Status updates come as a
labelled bullet list with bold lead-ins. Verdicts on other people's work are softened; praise is
flat and brief.

> Hi Tracy, yes, he was cc'd

> Hi Tracy, This sounds good. I like the topic, and especially that they're framing it as an
> education piece rather than a commercial pitch, which is exactly the right way round for our
> audience. Happy to include it once you've firmed things up with them. Best,

Long updates use the **labelled bullet** form: `**Hotel:**`, `**Sponsors and nominations:**`,
`**Regionals:**`, `**Workshop:**`, `**October:**`. One or two sentences each, ending with where
the item now sits and who holds it.

### Devianee, Swastee (delivery and admin colleagues)
Directive but courteous. No pleasantry. States the purpose, gives the material, says what he
wants done with it. "Please" plus imperative. Closes "Thanks,".

> Hi Devianee, For your records, here are COMELEC's 18 regional election directors, who I've just
> invited to the Symposium (Swastee and you were copied on the invitations). Please add them to
> the delegate tracking as responses come in.

> Hi Devianee, Swastee, can we please also get this email out – maybe stagger it with the webinar email?

### Internal colleagues at distance (Shane, Yogi, Arvind, Joana)
Pleasantry opener returns. Full reasoning, real numbers, and an explicit recommendation. He
explains the cost and the plan rather than asking permission in the abstract.

> Hi Shane, I hope you're well. I've built an AI application that does research for speakers for
> PPE and contacts for electoral, as well as collecting their emails. […] The pricing for it is
> £41 per month – that'll give us 2,000 tokens per month. I think this will be sufficient
> initially and then we may want to increase it, if we think it is useful.

Money and awkward internal asks are stated plainly, once, with the other side's position
acknowledged and no grovelling:

> I really need to receive full wage this month, and I would be grateful if some effort could be
> made towards reducing the outstanding balance as well. I understand finances continue to be
> tight, but any progress on this would be greatly appreciated.

### Senior officials (chairmen, commissioners, directors)
"Dear [Title] [Surname],". Institutional voice: the organisations act, not Jack. Short, formal,
deferential without being obsequious. No contractions in the most formal letters.

> Dear Chairman Garcia, Warm congratulations once again on your recognition as Electoral
> Commissioner of the Year at the 21st International Electoral Awards. Please find attached a
> formal letter from ICPS proposing to co-host an Electoral Symposium in the Philippines […]
> We look forward to the opportunity to collaborate with COMELEC.

> Dear Mr Balanon, Acknowledging receipt of the memorandum, with thanks. We're grateful to Deputy
> Executive Director Aguila-Flores for putting our request to the Chairman and Commissioner
> Ferolino, and we'll await their guidance.

Note the passive-institutional construction used for the formal invitation body: "The Commission
on Elections and the International Centre for Parliamentary Studies (ICPS) are co-hosting…",
then a plain-English exit: "just reply to this email and we will add you to the delegate list."

### Sponsors and commercial partners
Warm but businesslike. **Benefit-framed, never feature-listed.** Deadlines stated flatly with the
consequence attached. He personalises the greeting by closeness even within one mail-merge round:
the same nominations ask went out as "Dear Maryam", "Hi Rajeev", "Hi Martijn", "Dear Arnd",
"Hi Matthew".

> A nomination is a good way to put a strong deployment in front of the judges, and to recognise a
> partner's work beyond the project itself. […] The nomination form takes about ten minutes, and
> an organisation can be nominated in more than one category. If you have any questions, please ask.

Multi-question replies get the **"In order:" treatment**, each question as a bold label:

> Thank you for these, and apologies for the slow reply. In order:
> **Panel and presentation:** we would suggest day 2, Tuesday 1 December. […] That is the natural
> home for your panel and presentation.
> **Award category:** we allocate presentation categories much nearer the time […]
> **Advert and logo:** could you send the full-page advert artwork […] by the end of August?

### Speakers
His most elaborate register. He says **why this person specifically**, citing evidence, then
numbers the asks, then gives timings and next steps.

> Sierra Leone's responses highlighted some of the most pressing workforce challenges in our
> dataset — particularly around credential verification, training gaps, and the challenge of
> managing approximately 90,000 temporary workers with constrained infrastructure.

> To confirm your involvement, I would be grateful if you could share the following at your convenience:
> 1. A short speaker biography (100–150 words) for the event programme and introductions
> 2. A headshot or professional photo
> 3. Your preferred job title as it should appear on the event materials

Thank-yous after the event are warm and specific about the audience reaction, not about the speaker's brilliance:

> I thought it was a great session with some really valuable insight from all three of you. The
> audience clearly agreed. We had far more questions than we could get through in the time.

### Suppliers (hotels, venues)
Four lines. Thanks, the fact, the ask, sign-off. Never chatty, never curt.

> Hi Mafalda, Thanks for your help today. Unfortunately, Allan Best is no longer able to attend
> the event this week. Would it please be possible to cancel his room? Many thanks,

### Declines and bad news
Gives the actual reason, specifically, then offers a route forward. He does not hide behind
process.

> Unfortunately, on this occasion we're not able to proceed with your application. The brief was
> informative and gave a good outline of the current context, but I was looking for a brief that
> delved a little more into specific pieces of legislation and government schemes, thus a slightly
> more technical document as that tends to be our focus. If you're interested, I'd be happy to
> pass your details over to a sister organisation […]

When someone declines to him, he closes warmly and leaves the door open, with an exclamation mark
on the warm line:

> Hi Sead, many thanks for coming back to me – best wishes for your travel round Bosnia!
> Hopefully we may be able to work together in the future,

## Greetings and sign-offs

| Greeting | Used for |
|---|---|
| `Hi [First],` | Default. Anyone he has corresponded with, including senior externals |
| `Dear [First],` | Sponsors and contacts at slight distance, or a formal ask to someone he knows |
| `Dear [Title] [Surname],` | Senior officials, first contact ("Dear Chairman Garcia", "Dear Director Surmieda", "Dear Mr Koroma") |
| `Hi all,` / `Hi both,` | Small groups |
| `Dear speakers,` | Speaker cohort |
| `Dear Colleagues,` | Bulk sends |

| Sign-off | Used for |
|---|---|
| `Many thanks,` | The workhorse. Requests, and anything where the other person has done or will do something |
| `Kind regards,` | Formal, first contact, officials, sponsors, bulk |
| `Best,` | Familiar peers and internal |
| `All the best,` | Warm close, peers, ending a thread |
| `Best wishes,` | Warm-formal, speakers |
| `Thanks,` | Quick internal |
| `With thanks,` | Bulk and marketing |

He does not use "Sincerely", "Regards" alone, "Cheers", or "Warmly".

## Sentence-level habits

**The diminutive softener.** "Just" and "quick" carry most of his politeness load, and appear in
nearly every request: "just wanted to drop you a quick message", "a quick one on the inclusive
elections webinar", "Just a quick chaser", "a quick rundown", "a quick reminder", "a quick call",
"just a quick message from me and the ICPS team".

**Apologies come with the reason attached.** He apologises for delay very readily and always says
why, in one clause:

- "apologies for the slow reply"
- "apologies for the delay in coming back to you. I've been out of office for the last three days"
- "Apologies for my delay in coming back to you – it has taken a bit longer to confirm the hotel than expected"
- "my apologies that the introduction to COMELEC's administration office went quiet after March"
- "I apologise for an email on Saturday, unfortunately I have a last-minute request!"

**Asks are phrased as questions, not instructions.** "Would it please be possible to…",
"Would you potentially be able to…", "Could you please…", "Can I confirm that…",
"I would be grateful if you could…". The one imperative form he does use is with internal
delivery staff: "Please add them to…".

**The standard closing pair.** Almost every outbound email ends with an availability line then the
sign-off: "If you have any questions, please let me know." / "If you have any questions, please
ask." / "please do get in touch if you have any questions." / "If you have any questions in the
meantime, please do not hesitate to get in touch."

**Contractions** throughout, except in formal letters to chairmen and commissioners.

**Paragraphs run one to three sentences**, very often one. Single-sentence paragraphs are normal
and correct for him.

**Exclamation marks** are rare, roughly one per email at most, and land on the warm or
self-deprecating line, never the business one: "lovely to hear from you!", "best wishes for your
travel round Bosnia!", "I have a last-minute request!", "Ahh thanks Jorge, sorry was previously
just seeing the headshot!"

**Dashes.** In his own sending he uses spaced en dashes (` – `) freely. **Drafts prepared for him
must still avoid em dashes**, per the standing repo instruction; that is a house rule for
generated text, not a description of his habit.

## How the voice has changed, 2021 to 2026

**2020 to 2021 (Public Policy Exchange, "Policy Lead").** Longer and more hedged. Speaker
invitations paste the event's whole topic list as six bullets. More throat-clearing:
"I was wondering if you would be interested in speaking again?" Reads competent and junior.

**2022 to 2023.** Clipped and transactional. Dominated by venue, hotel and logistics threads.
Short paragraphs arrive; the pleasantry opener stays. Sales emails from this period are the least
characteristic thing in the corpus, and read off-brand against everything since:
"We're excited about the upcoming International Electoral Affairs Symposium…"

**2024.** More relational. Judges, sponsors and returning contacts. Warmer closings appear:
"Thank you once again for your invaluable contribution, and I look forward to working with you in
the lead-up to the event!"

**2025 to 2026 (ICPS, "Head of Policy Research").** Noticeably tighter and more confident. Shorter
sentences, semicolons for linked clauses, labelled sections in long replies, and reasons given for
recommendations rather than options offered. Benefit-framing replaces feature-listing. He now
states a view and stops:

> That is the natural home for your panel and presentation.

> We'd rather not run the session to low numbers when the line-up deserves a proper audience.

The 2026 sponsor and Manila emails are the best model to imitate. If a draft needs a reference
point, use those rather than anything before 2024.

## What he does not do

- Build the case. Point made, then stop. The explanatory sentence after a good point is the one he cuts.
- Stack reasons in an ask, or sell.
- Use corporate verbs: leverage, circle back, touch base, reach out (as a noun), as per.
- Superlatives about ICPS or its events.
- Chase aggressively. Chasers are "just a quick chaser" and give an easy out.
- Close with an either/or question to an internal colleague. He ends decisively with what he will do next.
